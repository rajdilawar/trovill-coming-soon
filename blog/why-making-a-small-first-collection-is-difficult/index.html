Why Making a Small First Collection Is Surprisingly Difficult

By Trovill · 10 November 2026

When we started working on Trovill, we expected to spend most of our time on the clothes. The fit of a T-shirt. The weight of a sweatshirt. Finding a navy that looked right next to the other colours in the collection.

We did spend time on those things. But we also spent months trying to find someone who could make them in quantities we could afford.

When you are funding a business with your own savings, that last part follows you into every conversation.

The emails nobody answers

We put together our lookbook, explained the brand and contacted manufacturers. Then we waited.

Most enquiries received no reply. We followed up, searched for more contacts and tried again.

Occasionally, an email arrived that looked promising. Someone had read the presentation. They were interested in the collection. They wanted to discuss production.

Then came the minimum order: 1,000 pieces.

Sometimes that minimum applied to a style. Sometimes to each colour. Either way, the conversation changed quickly.

A thousand pieces might be a normal production run for the factory. For us, it meant committing a substantial part of our budget before a single customer had tried the garment.

And we still had the rest of the collection to make.

A workable quantity wasn’t enough

Finding a supplier willing to accept a smaller order felt like progress. But we had other questions.

Where would the clothes be made? What could the supplier tell us about working conditions? Which certifications were current, and what did they actually cover? Could they provide the fabric and finishing we wanted?

Some conversations became less reassuring as we asked for more detail. Others ended because the price or quantities simply did not work.

We were looking for several things at once, and that made the search slow.

There were days when we wondered whether we were asking too much for a first collection. But these questions would eventually matter to the people buying our clothes. We needed to ask them before placing an order.

So we kept going.

There is another minimum behind the factory’s minimum

One of the more frustrating discoveries was that a factory could agree to a small garment order and still be unable to source the fabric for it.

The fabric has its own production requirements. A particular weight, construction or custom colour may need a separate run at the mill. That run has a minimum too.

Depending on the supplier and specification, custom fabric orders can require hundreds or thousands of metres. Adding another colour can mean another commitment, rather than simply dividing the same order between two shades.

Organic cotton is available in smaller quantities, including from stock. But stock means choosing from what already exists. The colour might work while the weight does not. The fabric might feel right, but there may not be enough available for production.

We had to weigh those options against the clothes we were trying to make.

Making the numbers work

Our costing sheets became as familiar as our designs.

Fabric, cutting, sewing, embroidery, labels, packaging, inspection, transport. Each line had to fit into a selling price that customers could understand and a margin that would allow us to continue.

Ordering more could reduce the price per garment. It could also leave us with money tied up in boxes of unsold stock.

Ordering less meant paying more for each piece.

We wanted to keep the cotton fabrics we had chosen rather than switch to polyester simply to bring down a quotation. That left us with more work to do on quantities, sourcing and the size of the collection.

Some ideas had to wait. That was difficult, especially after spending so long developing them.

Why we started with the T-shirt

We chose to focus our first organic cotton run on the T-shirt.

It is a piece people wear directly against their skin, wash regularly and reach for without much thought. For us, it felt like a worthwhile place to concentrate the available budget.

We wanted to give that everyday garment proper attention: its fabric, its fit, the neckline and how it holds up with wear.

This decision also gave us a manageable starting point. Trying to source organic cotton across every suitable piece, in every planned weight and colour, would have stretched the first collection further than we could responsibly fund.

We still want to expand its use. For now, we need to be clear about where we have started.

An organic cotton T-shirt does not make the whole collection organic. Each product needs its own accurate composition and supporting documentation. Customers should be able to understand what they are buying without having to interpret a broad claim about the brand.

What a small beginning really involves

From the outside, a first collection can look like a handful of clothes.

Behind it are enquiries that went nowhere, samples to assess, documents to check and calculations that changed after the next quotation arrived.

There is also the less visible work of staying patient. Returning to a supplier with another question. Revisiting a colour choice. Deciding what can wait until the next collection.

We are building Trovill with our own money, and we want to give it the chance to last. That means being ambitious about the clothes while being realistic about what we can make at the beginning.

We would love to have every part of our long-term vision ready for launch. We don’t.

What we can do is explain our choices, describe our materials accurately and take responsibility for the pieces we put our name on.

That is where our first collection begins.
